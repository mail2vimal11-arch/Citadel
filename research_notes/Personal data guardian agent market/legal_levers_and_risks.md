# Legal levers and legal risks for a consumer "personal data guardian agent" (as of 12 Nov 2026)

Method note for the report writer: full-page fetches were blocked this session for gowlingwlg.com, www.blakes.com, www.mcmillan.ca and www.edpb.europa.eu (hosts outside the session's allowed network domains), so I stopped fetching and relied on search-result extracts throughout. Almost every finding below is therefore from a search snippet, often of a law-firm or vendor summary and not the statute or regulator page itself. Items marked "(secondary)" or "(single source)" need a primary-source check before publication. This is background research, not legal advice.

## 1. Canada: individual rights, agent standing, enforcement, reform, credit-bureau rights

### Takeaway
Outside Quebec, Canada in force today gives a guardian agent modest leverage: PIPEDA grants access (30 days), correction and withdrawal of consent but no general erasure right, and the federal Commissioner cannot order or fine. Two things changed in 2026: Bill C-36 (introduced 15 June 2026, still only a bill) would add disposal and mobility rights, binding orders, penalties and a private right of action, and Ontario's credit security freeze came into force on 1 July 2026.

### Cited Findings
**PIPEDA (in force)**
- Organizations must respond to an access request as quickly as possible and no later than 30 days after receipt; an extension of at most 30 further days is allowed in limited cases, and the organization must tell the requester within the first 30 days and advise of the right to complain to the OPC — [OPC, Accessing your personal information – businesses](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/accessing-personal-information/api_bus/)
- Access is to be given free or at minimal cost — [OPC, PIPEDA access principle](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/p_principle/principles/p_access/)
- The OPC's own parliamentary briefing says its investigative recommendations are non-binding and that, unlike some provincial commissioners, it has no power to issue binding orders; unresolved matters go to Federal Court under PIPEDA ss. 14-15 — [OPC briefing to ETHI, Oct 2025](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-and-transparency-at-the-opc/proactive-disclosure/opc-parl-bp/ethi_20251006/is_20251006/)
- A June 2026 commentary on the OPC's Grok decision repeats that, despite finding PIPEDA violations, the OPC "cannot impose fines or issue binding orders" (secondary) — [MLT Aikins](https://www.mltaikins.com/?p=31253)
- The OPC found only a limited right to delist under PIPEDA (headline only; body not read) — [Mondaq](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/canada/privacy-protection/1693992/privacy-commissioner-of-canada-finds-limited-right-to-delist-under-pipeda)
- The OPC publishes an authorization form for a representative; the search extract describes it as covering a representative acting in a privacy complaint (form not opened) — [OPC authorization form](https://priv.gc.ca/media/2437/authorization_e.pdf)
- The OPC's guidance on who can act for others points to Alberta PIPA s. 61(1) and gives examples of representatives: legal guardians of incapable minors, personal representatives of an estate, and an attorney under a relevant power of attorney — [OPC](https://priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/r_o_p/02_05_d_26/)
- A Canadian law firm has published a client alert titled "Proliferation of Access Requests as New Tools Automate Request Generation and Distribution" (title only; fetch blocked) — [McMillan](https://www.mcmillan.ca/Privacy-Alert-Proliferation-of-Access-Requests-as-New-Tools-Automate-Request-Generation-and-Distribution)

**Federal reform (proposed, not in force)**
- Bill C-27 died on the order paper in January 2025 when Parliament was prorogued — [billc27.ca](https://billc27.ca/)
- Bill C-36, "An Act to enact the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act [PPCDA], to amend PIPEDA…", was introduced 15 June 2026 (45th Parliament, 1st session) — [Parliament of Canada, first reading text](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-36/first-reading); [ISED backgrounder](https://www.canada.ca/en/innovation-science-economic-development/news/2026/06/government-of-canada-introduces-legislation-to-protect-canadians-privacy-in-the-digital-age.html)
- Oversight would move to a Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada (a renamed Digital Safety Commission from Bill C-34) with a Privacy and Consumer Data Commissioner inside it; no AIDA-style AI statute is attached — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/canada-tables-bill-c36-the-protecting-privacy-and-consumer-data-act); [Blakes](https://www.blakes.com/insights/third-time-s-the-charm-canada-s-latest-approach-to-reform-the-federal-private-sector-privacy-framew/)
- Proposed administrative penalties up to $10 million or 3% of global revenue, and fines up to $25 million or 5% for indictable offences — [BLG guide](https://www.blg.com/-/media/insights/2026/documents/blg-federal-privacy-bill-c-36-guide-en.pdf); [MLT Aikins](https://www.mltaikins.com/insights/implications-of-canadas-proposed-privacy-overhaul-bill-c-36-for-organizations/)
- The Commission would have authority to make binding orders; commentary lists a right to disposal, data mobility and a right to explanation of automated decisions — [Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2026/06/bill-c-36); [McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/bill-c-36-what-organizations-need-to-know-about-canada-s-new-privacy-reform)
- Proposed private right of action for loss or injury, contingent on a finding of contravention by the Commissioner, Commission or a court (or a conviction), with a two-year limitation, in Federal Court or a provincial superior court (s. 132(5)); commentators expect follow-on class actions — [McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/bill-c-36-how-canada-s-proposed-privacy-law-could-increase-proposed-class-action-filings)
- Stage: secondary sources put it at second reading in the House as of October 2026 with no debate recorded; one says first reading. Not verified on LEGISinfo — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=3056e377-bd4f-454f-b609-e7b98a3979e1); [iWebServe](https://iwebserve.ca/articles/canadian-law/canadian-privacy-law-2026-bill-c36.php)
- The bill keeps a "substantially similar" mechanism for provincial laws — [Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2026/understanding-bill-c36)

**Quebec (Law 25, in force)**
- The modernized Private Sector Act has been fully in force since 22 September 2024, the last piece being data portability — [McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/quebecs-portability-regime)
- Portability covers computerized information collected from the individual, excludes created or inferred information, and applies unless it raises serious practical difficulties — [LW Law](https://www.lwlaw.com/quebecs-law-25-the-right-to-data-portability-under-quebecs-new-privacy-regime/)
- Individuals can require cessation of dissemination, de-indexation or re-indexation of information that causes serious injury to reputation or privacy (secondary) — [Termly](https://termly.io/resources/articles/quebec-law-25)
- The CAI can impose administrative monetary penalties up to C$10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover; penal fines go up to C$25 million or 4%, imposed by the Court of Québec — [Osler](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/updates/law-25-a-new-enforcement-scheme-for-protection-of-personal-information-in-the-private-sector-in-que/?pdf=1)
- Section 93.1 gives a private claim with punitive damages of not less than $1,000 where an infringement is intentional or results from gross fault — [Osler](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/updates/law-25-a-new-enforcement-scheme-for-protection-of-personal-information-in-the-private-sector-in-que/?pdf=1)
- Rights requests must be honoured within 30 days (s. 32), and identity checks should be proportionate to data sensitivity (secondary, single source) — [Hilotech Law 25 wiki](https://hilotech.ca/en/wiki/law-25/individual-rights)

**Alberta and BC PIPA (in force; reform pending)**
- BC's PIPA Regulation defines "representative" as a committee under the Patients Property Act, an attorney under an enduring power of attorney, a litigation guardian, or a representative under the Representation Agreement Act; such a representative may exercise s. 23 rights, make s. 24 requests and give or refuse consent — [BC PIPA Regulation 473/2003](https://free.bcpublications.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/473_2003)
- An Alberta OIPC order (P2019-07) dealt with an organization rejecting a doctor's letter as proof that a complainant was authorized to act for a family member — [Alberta OIPC Order P2019-07](https://dev2.oipc.ab.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Order-P2019-07.pdf)
- Alberta ran a public consultation on modernizing PIPA from 2 Feb to 1 May 2026; a legislative committee made 12 recommendations, including giving the OIPC power to impose administrative penalties. No amending bill was found — [Government of Alberta](https://www.alberta.ca/modernizing-albertas-personal-information-protection-act-albertan-survey); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/canada/privacy-protection/1744984/albertas-private-sector-privacy-law-consultation-on-potential-legislative-updates)

**Ontario**
- Ontario has no private-sector privacy statute of general application; a 2021 white paper proposed one (2021 source) — [Blakes](https://www.blakes.com/insights/new-ontario-consultation-on-private-sector-privacy/)
- Security-freeze provisions of the Consumer Reporting Act took effect 1 July 2026; timeliness requirements for lifting a freeze start 1 July 2027 — [Wikipedia, Credit freeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_freeze); [Consumer Reporting Act, e-Laws](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90c33)
- O. Reg. 168/25 sets deadlines: two business days for electronic freeze requests, 15 business days for mail — [O. Reg. 168/25](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/r25168)
- Only Equifax and TransUnion must offer freezes, free of charge — [Ontario.ca, Credit reports](https://www.ontario.ca/page/credit-reports)
- Equifax announced "Credit Lock" for Ontarians on 11 June 2026; one outlet says TransUnion has until 1 July 2027 for the suspension requirements (conflicts with the government page on bureau coverage) — [Equifax Canada](https://www.equifax.ca/about-equifax/newsroom/-/intlpress/equifax-canada-introduces-credit-lock-to-empower-ontarians-and-help-fight-identity-theft-and-fraud/); [Money.ca](https://money.ca/news/ontario-credit-lock-equifax-free-identity-fraud)

**Other credit-bureau rights**
- Quebec's free freeze has been live with both bureaus since 1 February 2023 (secondary) — [Credit Karma Canada](https://www.creditkarma.ca/credit/i/credit-freezes-expanding-in-canada)
- BC passed credit-freeze legislation (reported December 2025) that is not yet in effect; one source says August 2027. Nova Scotia is reportedly working on similar legislation (secondary, dates conflict) — [Frugal Flyer](https://frugalflyer.ca/blog/credit-freezes-in-canada/); [BC Gov News](https://news.gov.bc.ca/34129)

**CASL**
- CASL's private right of action was suspended indefinitely by Order in Council on 2 June 2017; regulatory enforcement continues, with maximum penalties of $1 million for individuals and $10 million for companies — [Bennett Jones](https://www.bennettjones.com/Insights/Blogs/Relief-as-Private-Right-of-Action-Suspended-Under-Canadas-Anti-Spam-Law-CASL); [Slaw](https://www.slaw.ca/?p=86214)

### Inferences
- PIPEDA has no express authorized-agent clause that surfaced; agent standing rests on ordinary agency plus whatever proof a company accepts. A company can lawfully insist on verifying authority, and there is no regulator penalty for stonewalling, so the agent's only escalation is an OPC complaint and then Federal Court.
- BC is the tightest: the regulation's closed list (committee, enduring power of attorney, litigation guardian, representation agreement) does not obviously cover a commercial agent holding a simple signed authorization. A workable design there is requests sent in the consumer's own name with the service as a drafting and tracking tool, or a power of attorney. Lawyer's opinion needed.
- Quebec is the only Canadian jurisdiction where a missed deadline carries real money risk for the company (AMPs, penal fines, s. 93.1 damages), which makes deadline tracking and CAI escalation credible there.
- In Ontario the service's privacy-rights levers are federal (PIPEDA), plus the new provincial freeze right. Freeze placement on a consumer's behalf is a concrete, statute-backed feature, but whether a bureau must accept an agent's request was not established.
- If C-36 passes roughly as tabled, Canada moves from weakest to mid-table: binding orders, penalties and a gated private right of action. It should be treated as upside, not a launch assumption; two predecessor bills died.

### Gaps
- PIPEDA's exact statutory wording on correction, withdrawal of consent and retention limits (Schedule 1 principles) was not retrieved this session; the absence of a general erasure right is widely stated but I have only the delisting headline as a cited proxy.
- No source found on whether Quebec's Act (s. 30) lets a mandatary or representative make requests, or what proof the CAI expects.
- Exact text of Alberta PIPA s. 61(1), including whether it covers "any person with written authorization", was not confirmed.
- C-36: no source on authorized-representative or minors provisions, coming-into-force timing, or committee schedule. Stage not verified on LEGISinfo.
- No 2025-2026 Ontario private-sector privacy bill found; PHIPA penalty details and any IPC statement were not retrieved.
- CASL's 10-business-day unsubscribe rule and any 2025-2026 CRTC enforcement were not confirmed this session.
- Fraud-alert law by province (e.g., Ontario, Manitoba) was not found.

## 2. EU and UK GDPR

### Takeaway
The GDPR gives the broadest rights and largest fines, and regulators accept that a third party can make requests with proof of authority, but controllers may bypass the agent's portal and answer the individual directly. Two 2026 developments cut against agents: the UK's "stop the clock" and proportionate-search rules are now law, and the EU Council's Digital Omnibus mandate (not law) would let controllers refuse access requests showing "abusive intention".

### Cited Findings
- GDPR Art. 12(3) sets a one-month response deadline (extendable by two months), and Art. 83 caps fines at EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide turnover (statute text; URLs not opened this session) — [Art. 12 GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-12-gdpr/); [Art. 83 GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-83-gdpr/)
- EDPB Guidelines 01/2022 on the right of access: version 2.0 adopted 28 March 2023 after consultation, version 2.1 with minor corrections 30 May 2024 — [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-012022-data-subject-rights-right-access_en)
- The EDPB summary says a third party can make an access request on behalf of another person — [EDPB summary, Sept 2025](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-09/right-of-access-guidelines-summary_en.pdf)
- Per Freshfields, the controller must ensure the proxy is legally authorised, need not use the third party's portal, and may disclose the data to the data subject another way; the guidelines are not legally binding (commentary written on the draft) — [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/the-edpb-further-shapes-requirements-for-data-subject-access-requests-102hhua)
- The consultation draft said controllers are not obliged to send the response to the portal; an academic submission criticised this as letting controllers override the data subject's chosen channel — [Arthur Cox](https://www.arthurcox.com/knowledge/edpb-draft-guidelines-on-data-subject-access-requests-dsars-key-points/); [González Fuster submission](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/webform/public_consultation_reply/feedback%20ACCESS%20ggf.pdf)
- ICO guidance: a controller can receive a SAR through a third-party portal but should be satisfied the portal acts with the person's authority and that the request can be viewed without paying or signing up; the third party must evidence its authority — [ICO, How do we recognise a SAR?](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/right-of-access/how-do-we-recognise-a-subject-access-request-sar)
- Art. 80(1) lets a data subject mandate a qualifying not-for-profit body to lodge complaints and exercise rights under Arts. 77-79 and 82; Art. 80(2) action without a mandate depends on national law — [GDPRhub, Art. 80](https://gdprhub.eu/index.php?title=Article_80_GDPR); [gdpr.eu, Art. 80](https://gdpr.eu/article-80-representation-of-data-subjects/)
- UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025: the main Part 5 data-protection changes commenced 5 February 2026 — [DLA Piper](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/02/uk-commencement-of-the-data-protection-provisions-in-the-data-use-and-access-act/)
- The Act limits SAR entitlement to what a "reasonable and proportionate" search finds, and lets controllers "stop the clock" while awaiting information they reasonably need from the requester — [Kennedys](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-commencement-dates-and-planned-guidance-for-2026); [Stevens & Bolton](https://www.stevens-bolton.com/insights/102mgxs/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-key-reforms-in-force/)
- Controllers needed a data-protection complaints process in place by 19 June 2026 (secondary) — [Markel](https://businesshub.uk.markel.com/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-what-the-february-and-june-2026-changes-really-mean-for-small-businesses/)
- EU Digital Omnibus: Coreper agreed the Council's mandate on 21 October 2026. Its Art. 12(5) text would allow a fee or refusal of an Art. 15 request "where an abusive intention on the part of the data subject… can be demonstrated", burden on the controller; it drops the Commission's lower threshold for "excessive" requests. Parliament had not voted as of 24 October 2026. Not law (secondary blogs) — [EU Internet Policy blog](https://euinternetpolicy.wordpress.com/2026/10/23/the-councils-digital-omnibus-mandate-what-it-would-change-in-the-gdpr/); [legal247](https://legal247.no/en/insights/digital-omnibus-gdpr/)

### Inferences
- GDPR leverage is high on substance (access, erasure, objection, portability, one month, real fines) but agent standing is by mandate, not by statute, so each controller can demand proof and reply to the individual directly. A product that routes replies to the consumer's own inbox fits the guidance better than a closed portal.
- The ICO's "no pay, no sign-up" condition means a controller can ignore a portal link that requires account creation. Requests should arrive as a complete email with the authority attached.
- Art. 80 is reserved to not-for-profit bodies; a for-profit guardian cannot use it for complaints. Partnering with a qualifying non-profit is the route for regulator escalation in the EU.
- The Omnibus "abusive intention" language is aimed at litigation-leverage requests, but volume automation by a paid agent is the sort of pattern controllers will cite. The burden staying on the controller is the mitigating point.

### Gaps
- Final v2.1 paragraph text on portals was not read (EDPB site blocked); the "controller need not use the portal" point is sourced from commentary on the draft.
- No regulator decision, court ruling or published experience specific to Mine or Rightly was found.
- CJEU case law on excessive requests (e.g., C-416/23) and the Representative Actions Directive's interaction with data rights were not retrieved.
- Whether the Digital Omnibus changes advanced in Parliament after 24 October 2026 is unknown; one source's claim of a May 2026 provisional agreement appears to concern the AI Act part only.

## 3. United States

### Takeaway
California is the only jurisdiction found where authorized agents are written into the statute and regulations, where a regulator has fined a company specifically for obstructing agents (Honda, 2025), and where a state-run one-stop deletion platform (DROP) now binds 650-plus data brokers. Other states mostly recognise agents for opt-outs only, about 12 require honouring Global Privacy Control, and no federal privacy law has passed.

### Cited Findings
**CCPA authorized agents**
- Under 11 CCR § 7063, for requests to know, delete or correct, a business may require the agent to show the consumer's signed permission, and may ask the consumer to verify identity directly or confirm the permission; this does not apply where the agent holds a Probate Code power of attorney, and a business cannot require a power of attorney as a condition of using an agent — [Cornell LII, 11 CCR § 7063](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/11-CCR-7063); [Byte Back](https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2021/03/ccpa-update-new-regulations-approved/)
- A draft requirement that agents register with the Secretary of State was removed as inconsistent with the statute — [CPPA FSOR appendix](https://cppa.ca.gov/meetings/materials/20230203_item4_fsor_appendix.pdf)
- For opt-out and limit requests a business may ask only for signed written permission and may not make the consumer confirm the agent directly. The CPPA's March 2025 order fined American Honda $632,500, partly for contacting consumers to confirm agent requests and for demanding excessive information on opt-outs (119 consumers asked for excess information, 20 denied) — [CPPA announcement](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250312.html); [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/cppa-settles-with-honda-over-alleged-privacy-violations/); [McDermott](https://www.mwe.com/?p=312466)
- The Honda order also requires agents to give their own contact details alongside the consumer's — [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/cppa-drives-home-enforcement-priorities-6252413)

**Delete Act and DROP**
- DROP regulations were adopted in September 2025 and approved in November; DROP opened to consumers 1 January 2026 and brokers had to start processing on 1 August 2026, checking at least every 45 days — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/californias-new-delete-request-tool-impacts-data-brokers-and-residents); [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/12/analyzing-the-california-delete-act-regulations/)
- An agent may assist only after CalPrivacy has verified the consumer's California residency, and the agent's full name, email and trade name must be disclosed — [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/12/analyzing-the-california-delete-act-regulations/); [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/getting-ready-to-use-the-drop)
- Penalty of $200 per unprocessed deletion request per day, plus fees and costs; no private right of action (secondary) — [DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/glossary/what-are-the-delete-act-and-drop/); [Coblentz](https://www.coblentzlaw.com/news/california-drop-deletion-requests-are-underway/)
- As of 28 August 2026: about 500,000 Californians registered, 654 brokers participating, about 25% of brokers reporting processed requests (secondary; no official release found) — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/compliance-wave-coming-data-brokers-brace-drop-deletion-requests-under-delete-act)
- SB 361, signed 8 October 2025, expanded broker registration disclosures — [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/12/analyzing-the-california-delete-act-regulations/)
- Before DROP, California brokers received 58,031,327 deletion requests in 2024, and 22 of 603 brokers rejected 90% of those they received (vendor analysis of filings) — [edhat](https://www.edhat.com/california/news/california-data-brokers-deletion-requests-drop/)

**Other states and opt-out signals**
- About 20 state comprehensive laws are in effect; 12 require honouring a universal opt-out signal (CA, CO, CT, DE, MD, MN, MT, NE, NH, NJ, OR, TX), while VA, UT, IA, IN, TN and KY do not (vendor trackers; lists differ by one state) — [CookieScript](https://cookie-script.com/privacy-laws/universal-opt-out-mechanisms-in-us-privacy-laws-gpc-requirements-by-state); [privacyterms.io](https://privacyterms.io/universal-opt-out-mechanisms-adoption-2026)
- Four more laws were reportedly signed in 2026 (Oklahoma, Alabama, Louisiana, Vermont), effective 2027-2028 (single vendor source) — [Consenteo](https://www.consenteo.com/knowledge-hub/legal/us_state_privacy_law_tracker_2026)
- In Texas and Oregon the only agent-submitted request a controller must honour is an opt-out (vendor source) — [Optery](https://www.optery.com/privacy-laws-texas-oregon-authorized-agent-provisions)
- California AB 566 (signed 8 October 2025) requires browsers to offer a built-in opt-out preference signal from 1 January 2027 — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/california-enacts-first-in-nation-law-requiring-web-browser-opt-out-preference-signal)

**Enforcement 2024-2026**
- CPPA and the attorneys general of California, Colorado and Connecticut ran a joint GPC compliance sweep in September 2025 — [Parker Poe](https://www.parkerpoe.com/news/2025/09/from-california-to-connecticut-regulators-increase-focus-on); [Sourcepoint](https://sourcepoint.com/blog/multi-state-privacy-investigation-targets-gpc-compliance-while-california-pushes-opt-me-out-act/)
- Reported penalties: Healthline $1.55M (California AG, July 2025), Tractor Supply $1.35M (CalPrivacy, 30 September 2025), Todd Snyder $345,178 (2025), all involving broken opt-outs; one tracker says 12 of 14 CCPA settlements since 2022 involved opt-out failures (vendor trackers) — [Clym](https://www.clym.io/blog/ccpa-penalties-and-fines-what-businesses-need-to-know); [privacyterms.io](https://privacyterms.io/ccpa-fines-and-penalties)

**Federal**
- The SECURE Data Act (H.R. 8413) was introduced 22 April 2026 by House Energy and Commerce Republicans; it would preempt state laws that "relate to" its provisions, including data-broker registries, has no private right of action, and lacked bipartisan support. No enactment or later movement found — [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/house-republicans-introduce-comprehensive-federal-privacy-bill-secure-data-act)

### Inferences
- California's rules create an asymmetry useful to an agent: opt-out and limit requests need only signed permission and cannot be bounced back to the consumer, while know, delete and correct requests can be. A product should lead with opt-outs and DROP and treat access as the hard case.
- DROP both helps and commoditises. Broker deletion in California is now free and state-run, so a paid agent's value there shifts to non-broker companies, non-California residents, follow-up and verification of results.
- Federal preemption is the main US legal risk to the business model: the SECURE Data Act as drafted would displace the state provisions (agents, DROP, registries) that give the agent standing.

### Gaps
- Text of 11 CCR § 7026 (opt-out via agent) was not read directly; the rule is sourced from Honda commentary.
- No state-by-state table of authorized-agent provisions was retrieved beyond Texas and Oregon; Colorado, Connecticut and others need a statute check.
- No enforcement action outside California specifically about ignored agent requests was found.
- No official CalPrivacy DROP statistics were found; counts are from law-firm and vendor pages.

## 4. Other regimes in brief (Brazil, Australia, India)

### Takeaway
Brazil and India both expressly contemplate someone acting for the data subject, but India's "consent manager" role is reserved to registered Indian companies, and Australia's rights-expanding second tranche is still an exposure draft.

### Cited Findings
- Brazil LGPD Art. 18: rights are exercised on express request of the data subject or a "legally constituted representative"; Art. 19 requires simplified confirmation immediately and a full statement within 15 days — [LGPD Art. 18 translation](https://lgpd-brazil.info/chapter_03/article_18); [LGPD Art. 19 translation](https://lgpd-brazil.info/chapter_03/article_19)
- Australia: the statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy commenced 10 June 2025. An exposure draft of the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026 (tranche 2) was out for consultation until 18 September 2026 and reportedly includes a direct right of action — [Hall & Wilcox](https://hallandwilcox.com.au/news/privacy-act-reforms-what-the-second-tranche-means-for-businesses/); [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/ru/insights/2026-09/the-draft-tranche-2-privacy-act-reforms-whats-there-whats-new-and-whats-missing)
- India: DPDP Rules were notified 13-14 November 2025; consent-manager registration (Rule 4) is scheduled to start 13 or 14 November 2026; a consent manager must be an India-incorporated company with minimum net worth of INR 2 crore, registered with the Data Protection Board; most substantive obligations apply from about May 2027 — [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules/); [InfosecTrain](https://www.infosectrain.com/blog/dpdp-consent-managers-explained-registration-and-integration-deadlines); [JSA](https://www.jsalaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/JSA-Prism-InfoTech-November-2025-DPDP-Rules.Final_.pdf)

### Inferences
- India is the clearest statutory model of a regulated consumer-side intermediary, but it is a licensed domestic role, not something a Canadian start-up can use as a launch lever.

### Gaps
- LGPD fine levels (commonly stated as 2% of Brazilian revenue, capped at R$50 million per infraction) were not sourced this session.
- Whether India's Board has opened consent-manager applications is unconfirmed; one source says Rule 4 awaits further notification.
- Whether Australia's tranche 2 has been introduced to Parliament since September 2026 is unknown.

## 5. How companies resist agent-submitted requests

### Takeaway
The documented pattern is friction, not outright illegality: redirecting agents to web forms, asking the consumer to re-verify, not confirming completion, and denying where not strictly obliged. Access requests fail far more often than opt-outs or deletions.

### Cited Findings
- Consumer Reports' early Permission Slip analysis (32 companies, 157 beta users, explicitly not representative) sorted companies by whether they needed more information the agent could supply or information only the consumer could supply; when action was needed, companies typically told CR to resubmit email requests through their web form — [CR Innovation Lab](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/an-early-look-at-how-companies-handle-ccpa-requests-submitted-by-authorized-agents/)
- CR's 2021 "Do Not Sell" agent study (21 companies) concluded agent provisions "hold promise" but need more rulemaking and norms; several companies left agents without confirmation — [CR study PDF](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/CR_AuthorizedAgentCCPA_022021_VF_.pdf); [CR press release](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/consumer-reports-study-finds-authorized-agents-can-empower-people-to-exercise-their-digital-privacy-rights-in-california)
- In CR's access-request pilot, volunteers submitted more than 200 requests through CR and fewer than a quarter succeeded; each volunteer verified phone and email and signed a document appointing CR as agent — [CR, Findings from helping 104 consumers](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/?p=5885); [CR article](https://www.consumerreports.org/privacy/authorized-agents-plan-to-make-it-easier-to-delete-your-data-a8655835448)
- CR later reported companies improved as request volume grew, and co-developed a Data Rights Protocol with nine organizations to standardise requests — [CR, Reflections on two years](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/reflections-on-two-years-of-operating-an-authorized-agent-service-and-launching-a-paid-upgrade/)
- CR transferred Permission Slip to DeleteMe in 2026 after processing nearly 5 million data-rights requests — [CR, Permission Slip's next chapter](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/permission-slips-next-chapter-with-deleteme/)
- Honda contacted consumers directly to confirm agent opt-outs and was penalised for it (see section 3) — [CPPA](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250312.html)
- Commenters on the DROP rules argued that missing agent-authentication provisions would enable unauthorized deletions — [CPPA, DROP written comments](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/drop_written_all_comments.pdf)
- In the UK, repeated requests for further copies after a portal download may be treated as manifestly unfounded or excessive, and controllers can now pause the clock pending clarification (see section 2) — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ico-releases-updated-right-of-access-guidance)

### Inferences
- The lawful resistance points are identity and authority verification; the grey ones are form redirection and silence. A guardian service needs per-company playbooks and a consumer-in-the-loop step for access requests.
- The fact that CR, a well-resourced non-profit, handed its agent app to a commercial operator suggests agent services are operationally heavy even where the law is favourable. This is my reading; CR's stated reasons were not retrieved.

### Gaps
- No 2025-2026 published compliance rate for agent requests was found.
- CR's post on two years of operating was not read in full, so its specific lessons on verification refusals are unconfirmed.
- No evidence was found on how Canadian or EU companies respond to bulk agent requests in practice.

## 6. Legal exposure of the guardian service itself

### Takeaway
The service would hold identity documents, account lists and signed authorities, so it carries the same privacy-law duties it enforces, plus four distinct regulatory risks: unauthorized provision of legal services in Ontario, deceptive-efficacy and subscription claims, payments registration if it issues cards, and telecom registration if it issues numbers. Automated account access on third-party sites remains legally unsettled.

### Cited Findings
**Legal services**
- Ontario's Law Society Act s. 1(5): a person provides legal services if they engage in conduct involving "the application of legal principles and legal judgment with regard to the circumstances or objectives of a person"; s. 1(6) then lists specific activities, including giving advice on legal position and preparing documents — [Law Society Act, e-Laws](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90l08/v26); [vLex consolidation](https://ca.vlex.com/vid/law-society-act-r-1027722544)
- The LSO's Access to Innovation (A2I) sandbox lets approved providers of "innovative technological legal services" serve consumers for a set period under risk-based conditions and later receive ongoing permits; Willful was the first approved participant (November 2022) — [LSO A2I page mirror](https://c-3682-20130603-www-lsuc-on-ca.i.icims.com/about-lso/access-to-innovation/about-a2i); [Law Times](https://lawtimesnews.com/news/general/law-society-of-ontario-launches-access-to-innovation-project/361514)
- FTC v. DoNotPay: the final order (approved 5-0 on 16 January 2025, announced 11 February 2025) requires $193,000 in monetary relief, notice to 2021-2023 subscribers, and bars claims that the service performs like a real lawyer without evidence — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87474); [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2296777)

**Marketing and subscriptions**
- LifeLock paid $12 million in 2010 ($11M to the FTC, $1M to 35 states) over deceptive identity-theft-protection claims, then $100 million in 2015 for violating that order, including continued deceptive claims and data-security failures — [FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2015/12/lifelock-pay-100-million-consumers-settle-ftc-charges-it-violated); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-announces-largest-settlement-ever-with-lifelock)
- The FTC's 2024 click-to-cancel rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025; the FTC issued a new ANPRM on 11 March 2026 and no replacement rule has been adopted. ROSCA, FTC Act s. 5 and state auto-renewal laws still apply — [Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/02/us-ftc-signals-renewed-interest-in-click-to-cancel-rulemaking); [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses)
- Canada's Competition Tribunal imposed a record $38.9 million penalty on Cineplex in 2024 for drip pricing — [BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/economics/2026/01/30/competition-bureau-needs-to-keep-foot-on-gas-amid-economic-headwinds-commissioner/)
- In February 2026 the Competition Bureau and OPC jointly highlighted manipulative digital design, including cancellation obstruction — [Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2026/dark-patterns-haunting-digital-marketing)
- BC rules now bar automatic renewal of subscription contracts without advance notice — [Consumer Protection BC](https://www.consumerprotectionbc.ca/news/news-release-stronger-protections-against-automatic-contract-renewals-high-pressure-direct-sales-in-force/)

**Privacy and breach**
- The Quebec penalties and s. 93.1 damages in section 1 apply to any enterprise handling Quebec residents' data — [Osler](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/updates/law-25-a-new-enforcement-scheme-for-protection-of-personal-information-in-the-private-sector-in-que/?pdf=1)
- C-36 would add federal penalties and a class-action gateway (proposed) — [McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/bill-c-36-how-canada-s-proposed-privacy-law-could-increase-proposed-class-action-filings)

**Payments**
- Under the RPAA a payment service provider must be registered with the Bank of Canada before performing retail payment activities; new entrants apply at least 60 days ahead; the public registry launched October 2025 — [BDO](https://www.bdo.ca/insights/rpaa-compliance-in-canada-what-payment-service-providers-need-to-know); [NCFA](https://ncfacanada.org/bank-of-canadas-psp-registry-goes-live-under-rpaa/)
- Bank of Canada guidance lists prepaid cards as "holding funds", which triggers safeguarding in a segregated trust or insured account — [Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/end-user-funds-safeguarding/)
- An entity acting as agent of a registered PSP need not register itself — [Bennett Jones](https://www.bennettjones.com/Blogs-Section/Retail-Payment-Activities-Act-A-Deeper-Dive-Into-When-Registration-is-Required)
- Firms meeting both definitions must comply with FINTRAC's money-services-business regime and the RPAA — [Blakes](https://blakes.com/insights/regulation-of-retail-payments-in-canada-the-retail)

**Telecom**
- CRTC guidance says local VoIP providers must provide 9-1-1 service, register as a reseller or CLEC, and hold a BITS licence if carrying international traffic — [CRTC](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/phone/911/voip.htm)

**Automated agents and third-party terms**
- Amazon v. Perplexity: in March 2026 a district court enjoined Perplexity's Comet agent from accessing password-protected Amazon accounts, reasoning access was "with the Amazon user's permission, but without authorization by Amazon" (CFAA and California law). In August 2026 the Ninth Circuit vacated the injunction, reasoning the user, acting through the agent, accessed the system. The case continues — [GeekWire](https://geekwire.com/2026/judge-blocks-perplexitys-ai-bot-from-shopping-on-amazon-in-early-test-of-agentic-commerce/); [Retail Insight Network](https://www.retail-insight-network.com/newsletters/us-court-overturns-amazon-injunction-perplexity)
- Amazon updated its Business Solutions Agreement effective 4 March 2026 to require AI agents to identify themselves (secondary) — [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/amazon-perplexity-comet-injunction/)

### Inferences
- Sending a statutory access or deletion request on a template is arguably clerical; choosing which legal ground applies to a person's situation, drafting demand letters and writing regulator complaints moves toward "application of legal principles and legal judgment" under s. 1(5). This is the single most important question for an Ontario founder and needs a written opinion from an Ontario lawyer; A2I is the formal route if the answer is uncertain.
- The DoNotPay and LifeLock orders show the FTC's theory: the exposure is in over-claiming (acts like a lawyer, prevents identity theft, removes your data everywhere), not in the service category itself. Efficacy claims should be backed by measured results.
- Issuing virtual cards directly would likely make the service a PSP holding funds; operating as an agent of a registered PSP or card-issuing partner is the lighter path. Counsel needed on structure.
- Email aliases are low risk; virtual phone numbers pull in CRTC obligations if the service is the provider, so reselling a registered carrier's product is the lighter path.
- Logging into consumer accounts with an automated agent is unsettled law in the US and untested in Canada. Email and web-form requests under statutory rights carry far less risk than credentialed account access. Stand-in contact details may also breach individual sites' terms where real identity is required (e.g., financial services); no source was found on this.

### Gaps
- Exact text of Law Society Act s. 1(6) and the s. 1(8) exemptions was not confirmed; no LSO statement on privacy-request or complaint-drafting services was found.
- No US UPL ruling against a data-rights agent was found; DoNotPay's private UPL suits were not researched.
- Agency and power-of-attorney formalities (e-signature validity, revocation, minors) under Ontario law were not researched.
- Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2023 in-force status and subscription rules were not found; current Competition Act penalty maximums were not confirmed.
- Whether virtual single-use cards issued through a sponsor bank fall within an RPAA exclusion was not answered.
- Canadian breach class-action exposure levels and cyber-insurance expectations were not researched.

## 7. Regulatory tailwinds that create demand

### Takeaway
Demand drivers are real and rising: age-assurance mandates are multiplying identity-data collection, breach volumes are large, data-broker registration is expanding, and California has shown that hundreds of thousands of people will use a deletion tool when it is simple.

### Cited Findings
- The OPC's 2025-26 annual report reportedly records nearly 700 breach reports from businesses affecting more than 20 million Canadians (secondary); the prior year had 686 private-sector breach reports — [Lexpert](https://www.lexpert.ca/news/technology-law/federal-privacy-commissioners-report-tackles-strategic-priorities-in-data-driven-landscape/392714)
- A Lexpert headline reports a 109 percent increase in PIPEDA complaints in the Commissioner's annual report (headline only) — [Lexpert](https://www.lexpert.ca/news/technology-law/federal-privacy-commissioners-annual-report-notes-109-percent-increase-in-pipeda-complaints/394317)
- UK Online Safety Act age-verification duties have been enforced since July 2025; Australia's minimum social-media age took effect 10 December 2025; roughly half of US states require some age gating — [Jumio](https://www.jumio.com/age-verification-laws/); [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/are-new-global-age-verification-requirements-creating-a-children-s-online-safety-legal-patchwork-)
- Canada has no general age-verification law in force; Bills S-209 and C-34 reportedly reached House committee in late October 2026, and the OPC published age-assurance guidance in May 2026 (vendor and law-firm sources) — [Didit](https://didit.me/de/blog/age-verification-canada/); [MLT Aikins](https://www.mltaikins.com/?p=32288)
- Data-broker registries exist in California, Vermont, Texas and Oregon, with New Jersey and Connecticut added per 2026 guides; a 2025 PRC/EFF analysis found 750 brokers registered in at least one state and hundreds not registered across states — [Privacy Rights Clearinghouse](https://privacyrights.org/resources-tools/reports/why-are-hundreds-data-brokers-not-registering-states); [DataGrail](https://www.datagrail.io/?p=14058)
- DROP reached roughly 500,000 registrations within eight months of launch (secondary) — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/compliance-wave-coming-data-brokers-brace-drop-deletion-requests-under-delete-act)
- Permission Slip facilitated nearly 5 million requests before its 2026 transfer to DeleteMe — [CR Innovation Lab](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/permission-slips-next-chapter-with-deleteme/)
- GDPR Art. 80 and India's consent-manager regime are existing legal recognitions of representation by a third party — [GDPRhub](https://gdprhub.eu/index.php?title=Article_80_GDPR); [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules/)

### Inferences
- Age assurance is a double tailwind: it increases the amount of identity data held by third parties and gives a guardian product a concrete monitoring and deletion job (the Canadian bills reportedly require destruction of age-check data).
- Canada has no data-broker registry and no DROP equivalent, so there is no ready-made target list or one-stop mechanism in the home market.

### Gaps
- Exact OPC 2025-26 breach and complaint figures were not read from the OPC report itself.
- No Canadian data-broker registry proposal was found.

## 8. Ranked view: where a guardian agent has most leverage, and the most favourable launch market

### Takeaway
On law alone, California gives an agent the most enforceable standing, the EU and UK give the widest rights with weaker agent mechanics, and Quebec is the only Canadian jurisdiction with real consequences for companies. Ontario and the rest of PIPEDA Canada are currently the weakest on enforcement but would improve materially if Bill C-36 passes.

### Cited Findings
- All findings relied on here are cited in sections 1-7; this section is synthesis.

### Inferences
Ranking by legal leverage for an agent today (in force only):
1. **California.** Statutory agent role; regulations limiting what companies may demand; a fine specifically for obstructing agents (Honda); DROP with per-request daily penalties; mandatory GPC; browser signal mandate from 2027. Weaknesses: residency-gated, access requests still bounce to the consumer, DROP is free and reduces willingness to pay, federal preemption risk.
2. **EU and UK.** Fullest rights set, one-month deadline, largest fines, agents accepted with proof of authority. Weaknesses: no statutory agent role for a for-profit, controllers may bypass the portal, UK stop-the-clock, pending EU "abusive intention" amendment.
3. **Quebec.** GDPR-like rights, 30-day deadline, large penalties, minimum punitive damages. Weakness: agent standing and proof rules not confirmed this session.
4. **Other US states with universal opt-out (CO, CT, TX, OR and others).** Agent role mostly limited to opt-outs.
5. **Rest of Canada under PIPEDA, plus Alberta and BC.** Access, correction and withdrawal exist; no general erasure; federal regulator cannot order or fine; BC's representative list is narrow.
6. **Brazil, India, Australia.** Representation recognised in Brazil; India's role is licensed and domestic; Australia's individual rights expansion is still draft.

Launch-market view:
- **Legally most favourable for the core request-agent feature: California (US).** It is the only market where the product's central act is expressly authorized and regulators have enforced it.
- **Most practical for an Ontario founder may differ.** A Canadian launch has weaker levers but lower regulatory surface (one privacy regime to comply with, no FTC or state-AG exposure, a new Ontario freeze right to build on) and positions the product ahead of C-36. A Quebec-inclusive Canadian launch adds the strongest domestic lever but also the strongest penalties on the service itself and French-language obligations (not researched).
- Either way, the service's own Ontario-law questions (legal-services licensing, agency instrument, payments and telecom structure) apply regardless of where its customers live.

Lawyer's opinion needed before launch on: (a) Law Society Act s. 1(5)-(6) and whether A2I is required; (b) form and proof of agency per jurisdiction, especially BC and Quebec; (c) RPAA and FINTRAC status of any card feature; (d) CRTC status of any number feature; (e) CFAA and Canadian equivalents for any credentialed account access; (f) marketing claims substantiation.

### Gaps
- The ranking weighs law, not market size, willingness to pay or competition; those sit with other research streams.
- C-36's final form and timing are unknown; the ranking would change if it passes with binding orders and penalties intact.
